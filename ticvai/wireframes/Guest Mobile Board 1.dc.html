This app is white-labelled. It carries the venue's name and identity throughout — the frames below show Riverside — with a "powered by TICVAI" mark in four agreed positions only — first run, the home screen, the listing footer and the account page — and nowhere else. Two of the four appear on this board. The app is used in three states: at home deciding, on the way, and inside a building with no signal. Most ticketing apps are built for the first and fall apart in the third. Everything on this board assumes a phone held in one hand, a guest who has not opened the app for four months, and a network that may vanish at the door.

The home screen changes shape
With a ticket for tonight it is a ticket screen with a countdown. With a ticket for next month it is a short list of upcoming visits. With no tickets at all it is what's on, filtered to things this guest has previously chosen. Three layouts, one screen, decided by what the guest actually has.

Six facts per event, in the same order
Title, dates, room, running time, price from, and availability in words. Then the access and content flags, which are the two things guests most often have to phone about. A card that leads with a photograph and hides the date is a poster, not a listing.

Content notes are not a warning list
Strobe, smoke, loud noise, haze and interval timing are stated plainly because they are practical: a guest with epilepsy or a hearing aid needs the fact, not a paragraph of caution. Anything a guest would want to know before sitting down goes in this panel.
Written once per production by the company manager and read from the same record the front-of-house brief uses.
