The four dangerous permissions
Publish a seat map
Can invalidate sold seats. 14 people hold it; 6 have never used it.
Approve a price grid
Two signatures required, so no single person can move a price.
Issue comps
Against a named budget, always with a reason.
Reseat a sold booking
Allowed widely, because refusing it at the door costs more.
Six people can publish a map and never have. Removing it from those six costs nothing and halves the number of people who could accidentally sell a seat that does not exist.

Roles, not individual grants
Nobody is given a permission personally. If a job needs something the role does not have, the role changes and everybody doing that job gets it — which forces the question of whether the job really needs it.
Reviewed twice a year
The list of who holds a dangerous permission goes to the operations director in April and October. It takes ten minutes and catches every leaver.

The Citybreak feed has been rejecting updates for nine days and nobody was told, because it fails by returning a success code with an error in the body. Four guests bought seats that were already sold.
Failing loudly
Every connection has a heartbeat and a maximum silence. If no successful exchange happens within that window the integration is marked failed, the stated degradation applies, and somebody named is told. Nine days of silence should have raised an alert after fifteen minutes.

Degradation is a design decision
Each row states what happens to seats when that connection dies. "Sales continue, profiles stale" is a choice; so is "online stops". Neither should be discovered during an incident.
Nothing blocks the door
Every integration can be down and the building can still open: the door list is on the devices, and scanning decides locally. That is the one rule this page enforces absolutely.

The missing-attribute alert fires 208 times a day to the same two people and has never been acted on. It should be one weekly summary naming the room, which is exactly what the survey backlog page already is.
Rules for an alert existing
Somebody can act on it now
If the only response is "note it for later", it belongs on a page, not in an alert.
It names a person
Never a team address. Twelve alerts, twelve named recipients.
It fires rarely enough to be read
Anything more than a few times a week becomes a digest.
Its action rate is measured
Below 10% for a quarter and the alert is changed or deleted.
Measuring whether an alert was acted on is the only way to stop a notification system quietly becoming noise that everybody filters.

The most sensitive thing in this module is a list of people's access requirements. It is visible to the fewest staff, held only while somebody is a member, deletable on request, and every single view of it is logged and shown to the guest if they ask.
What we chose not to hold
No medical evidence, no diagnosis, no free-text history written by staff about a guest, and no record of which access need was "verified". The house decided that a guest saying they need a wheelchair space is sufficient, which removes an entire category of sensitive data and the arguments that go with it.
