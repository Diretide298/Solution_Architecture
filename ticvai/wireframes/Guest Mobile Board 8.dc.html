The venue name is stop 6, not stop 1. A guest listening for their seat should not have to hear the branding first.
Reading order is a design decision
Left to its own devices the reader would announce the venue logo, then the production, then work down. The order here is authored: ticket facts, then the five actions, then the branding. It reverses the visual hierarchy on purpose, because a guest with a reader is looking for one thing and a guest with eyes is orienting.

A row-and-number seat read as a string is unintelligible; every seat label carries a spoken form alongside the printed one.
Tested with guests, not with a linter
Automated checks pass on screens that are still unusable — a correct label in the wrong order is worse than no label. Each of these five screens was walked through with four regular guests who use a reader daily, and the changes that came out of it were about order and wording rather than markup.

Fewer things, bigger, rather than the same things smaller
At 200% the seat and door stack into one column, the four secondary actions move behind a single button, and the seat number grows to 34 points. The alternative — keeping the layout and shrinking the type back — is the thing large-type users complain about most, and the platform does not allow a tenant theme to do it.

At the largest setting the screen is the seat, the door and one button — which is all the ticket has ever needed to be.
Contrast is checked against the tenant palette
Every tenant colour pair is tested at the sizes it is used at, and a theme that fails is rejected at the point the venue saves it rather than discovered by a guest. Riverside's teal on white passes at 12px; the same teal as body text at 11px does not, so the platform will not permit it.

The brand steps aside at the door
Teal on dark green is legible indoors and useless in direct sun. On the door screen the tenant palette is reduced to a single line of small text and everything functional is black on white — the one place in the app where the white-label identity is deliberately suppressed, because a guest cannot read their seat is a worse outcome for the venue than a screen that does not look like theirs.

Brightness on request, not silently
The app asks before turning the screen to full brightness rather than doing it on arrival, because an unexpected flare at a dark auditorium door is unpleasant and drains a battery a guest may need for a train home.

The tannoy has a written channel, always
Front of house type nothing extra: the announcement panel on the duty manager's tablet is the tannoy, and pressing "interval ends in five" both plays the call and pushes the text. A venue that has to remember to also send a message will forget on the night it matters, so the two are one action.

An evacuation call also names the nearest exit for the guest's own seat, which the tannoy cannot do.
Useful to more than deaf guests
Fourteen per cent of guests open the announcement list at some point, far more than the number who are deaf or hard of hearing. A tannoy in a stone foyer is unintelligible to most people, and the written version is simply better for everybody.
