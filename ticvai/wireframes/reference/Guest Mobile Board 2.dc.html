Tonight is a different kind of row
The ticket for the next few hours is a card with the seat, the door and one large button; everything further out is a quiet line of text. A wallet that treats a visit in December the same as one in forty minutes makes the guest hunt at the exact moment they cannot.

Both tickets, stacked, no swiping
A carousel that hides ticket two behind a swipe is the single most common cause of a stalled door queue. Stacking them means the usher scans, the guest scrolls a thumb, and the usher scans again — about four seconds for a pair. Above six tickets the screen switches to a scan-and-advance mode that marks each one as it is read.

What happens
Sam gets their own ticket for N3 with a new barcode. Yours for N3 stops working straight away. You keep N2 and the booking stays in your name.
If Sam has access needs, ask them to tell us — N3 is a step-free seat two in from the aisle.

A transfer is a reissue, not a screenshot
Forwarding a PDF produces two people at one seat and an argument at the door. Reissuing voids the original barcode the moment the new one is created, so the door list shows Sam against N3 — which matters for the evacuation card as much as for admission.

Nearly a third of recipients came back and booked themselves. A transfer is a marketing channel that costs nothing.
Asking about access, carefully
The sender is told what kind of seat it is rather than asked to declare anything about the recipient. Sam is then asked directly, by us, when they claim the ticket — which is the only person who should be answering that question.
The seat's step count and aisle position travel with the ticket, so the new holder sees the same facts the buyer did.

The seat number is the collection code
A guest says "N2" faster than they spell a surname across a loud bar, and bar staff can lay trays out in seat order before the interval starts. Help that works without help
Every answer here was cached with the ticket: where the nearest usher stands, how far the accessible toilet is, tonight's timings, and re-entry. The last resort is a phone call over the mobile network rather than a chat window over data, because a stone auditorium kills data long before it kills a voice call.

Reporting a seat, queued
"My seat has a problem" writes a report locally with the seat number and sends it when signal returns, which is usually as the guest reaches the foyer. It lands in the same incident list an usher's tablet feeds, so the seat is out of sale for tomorrow without anybody typing an email.
