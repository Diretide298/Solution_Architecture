Held seats are the quietest way a venue loses money. Somebody blocks a row for a production that ends up not needing it, and nobody releases it because nobody owns it. Every hold in this module has three things a spreadsheet never gives it: a named owner, an expiry date, and a reason that can be checked. Nothing can be held indefinitely, and nothing is released without the person who asked for it being told.

The last hour is the whole point
Every hold in this module exists to be released, and most of what comes back arrives in the last sixty minutes: press no-shows, uncollected comps, declined cards. Fourteen people are standing at the desk. The duty manager needs one number and one button, not a seat map.

The online waiting list is offered first for releases more than two hours out; inside two hours the queue at the desk wins, because they are already here.
Why keep two back
Two house seats cover the things that go wrong after doors: a broken seat, a guest in the wrong section, a party split by a reseat, an usher needing to move somebody quietly. Selling them for £36 and then having nothing to offer a guest whose seat is unusable is a bad trade.
