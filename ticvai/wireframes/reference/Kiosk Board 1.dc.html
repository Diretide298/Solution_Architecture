Frame ids here are the client's reference codes. KSK-004 is the anchor, the label and the index link, so no mapping has to be agreed. The kiosk serves somebody standing in a queue who has already decided to come in. Every screen answers one question and offers one way forward. Controls sit in the 900–1400 mm reach band; the top third of the glass is never interactive.

The loop earns its place or it goes dark
Availability updates every 30 seconds from the same inventory the web storefront reads. If the venue has nothing left tonight the loop says so plainly and stops inviting touches — a kiosk that takes a queue's attention and then says "sold out" four screens later is worse than a dark screen.
Touch anywhere
The whole glass is the button in this state. Guests touch the poster, not the call to action; requiring an accurate first tap loses people who have never used the machine before.
Reach band
On a 1920-tall portrait screen mounted at 1150 mm, the seated reach band covers the bottom 62% of the glass. Everything above is display only — which is why the availability list, not a control, occupies it.

Collect is second, not first
Measured across comparable venues, buying is roughly two thirds of kiosk sessions and collection one third — but collection is the faster job, so it sits within one tap and its own path (KSK-011) skips every purchase screen.
No sign-in anywhere on this board
A kiosk purchase is anonymous by default. The guest is offered a receipt by email at the end, not an account at the start.
Timeout returns, never resets silently
45 seconds of no touch shows a 10-second "still there?" panel before returning to the loop. Nothing is charged, nothing is held.

The wheelchair row is never hidden
It appears whenever the session has an accessible space free, at the same size as every other row, with the companion policy on the row. A tenant can rename it; they cannot bury it behind an accessibility menu.
42 px steppers, 11 px apart
Above the 44 px minimum once the glass bezel offset is applied, and far enough apart that a mistaken decrement is rare. Quantity is capped at 8 per type; above that the screen offers staff help instead of a longer list.
Price is the last thing to move
The total sits directly above Continue and animates only on change, so a guest checking their maths does not have to look for it. Fees, if the tenant charges any, are named here and never added later.

Remaining counts are honest or absent
"6 left" means six sellable seats for the quantity in the basket, not six in total. If the tenant would rather not publish counts, the tile shows availability as a word — never an invented scarcity figure.

Seats are allocated, not picked
Best-available runs on selection; there is no seat map on the kiosk. A guest who wants a specific seat is directed to staff or the web storefront, where the map exists.

The hold is the same mechanism the web storefront and the app use. Showing the timer on the dark bar rather than in a corner is what stops a guest wandering off to find their card and losing the seats silently.

The reader is described by position
"Below the reader on your right" — because on a floor-standing kiosk the reader is not where a guest looks first. The dashed outline on screen sits on the same side as the physical hardware.
Cancel is always available until authorisation
Once the reader takes over, cancel greys and the copy changes to "follow the reader". A button that looks live but does nothing is how guests end up tapping twice.
Every ambiguous outcome goes to one place
Timeout, partial authorisation, reader disconnect and unknown result all land on KSK-008 rather than a generic decline, because the guest's real question is whether they have been charged.

Your seats stay held until a member of staff releases or confirms them. Nothing expires while you wait.
"Do not tap again" is the whole point
Double charges at kiosks come almost entirely from a guest retrying an ambiguous state. The screen removes the retry, holds the seats indefinitely, and hands the problem to a person.
The reference is short and spoken aloud
Four digits and a kiosk letter, formatted to be read out. Staff look it up on the handheld app; the same reference appears in the POS audit log.
The hold stops expiring
A five-minute timer running while a guest waits for staff would be an unforced error. Entering this state converts the hold to staff-released.

The guest bought at a machine at the door; they are walking in now. Asking for an email before the ticket prints is the single most common way kiosk queues stall.
The screen repeats what the paper says
Seats, door, step-free route. A guest with poor near vision can read the 18 px screen text where they cannot read thermal print.
