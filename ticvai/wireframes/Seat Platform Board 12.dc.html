Most seat reporting answers a question nobody asked: how full was it. The useful questions are harder — which seats never sell, what a held seat cost, where the discount actually went, and whether the survey work paid for itself. Every figure on this board traces back to a specific seat on a specific night, so a number can always be turned into a decision about the map, the price grid or a hold.

Standby is the only line where the discount probably was not needed. Sixty-one per cent of standby sales were on performances that later sold out anyway, which means £42 000 of it was given to people who would have paid more.
Fixing standby without breaking it
Standby exists so that somebody who cannot afford £58 can see the show, and so the last few seats are filled. Both purposes are served by offering it only on performances below 85% sold at the one-hour mark, rather than every night. On last season's data that keeps 2 600 of the 4 200 standby sales and recovers £42 000.

How attribution works
Each figure is the difference between the seats affected by one survey task and comparable seats that were not surveyed, over the same performances. It is not a model: 208 seats went from unsellable to sellable on a known date, and their income is countable. Where a claim could not be traced to specific seats, it is marked "enabling" and given no number.

The definitions everything uses
Sold
Paid for and not refunded, at the price actually paid including fee, on the date of payment.
Attended
Scanned into the building. Never inferred from a sale.
Available
On sale to the public at that moment. Excludes kills, includes held seats only once released.
Capacity
Seats that physically exist and are usable tonight. Not the number on the licence.
Four definitions, written down once, used by every screen in this module and every export leaving it. Most reporting arguments in a venue are really arguments about one of these four.
