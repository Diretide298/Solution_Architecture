Choosing a seat online is the moment all the survey work pays off. A guest is deciding between two rectangles on a phone screen, and what turns that into confidence is not a prettier map but the facts underneath it: how far back, how many steps, what is in the way, and a photograph taken from the seat. Everything here works at 390 pixels wide first, because two thirds of seat selections happen on a phone held in one hand.

Two taps, not a pinch-zoom

Pairs, not seats
A guest buying two tickets is choosing a pair, so the interface offers pairs. Tapping either seat of a highlighted pair takes both, and single seats that would leave an awkward gap are still selectable but not suggested. This one decision removes most of the fiddly tapping that makes mobile seat selection unpleasant.

Access is a filter, not a phone number
"No steps at all", "aisle seat" and "wheelchair space" sit beside "full view only" and a price cap, because to the guest they are the same kind of requirement. Putting them here rather than on a separate access page is what moved wheelchair space bookings from 54% online to 100%.
Counts, live
Every filter shows how many seats it leaves. A guest who taps four filters and gets zero results has wasted four taps; a guest who watches the number fall from 523 to 86 knows exactly which condition is expensive.

Never a zero-result dead end
If a combination leaves nothing, the screen says which single filter to drop and how many seats that would give back — "remove 'aisle seat' for 86 seats" — rather than showing an empty map and an apology.
Filters are also remembered for the next visit, so a guest who never uses stairs does not re-state it every time.

Offline is not optional
A foyer with two thousand people in it has no usable mobile signal. The ticket, the barcode, the seat and the door are stored on the device when the ticket is issued, so nothing about getting into the building depends on a network. Everything else on the screen — the countdown, the interval time — is a nice extra that degrades quietly.

Never send them back to the map
The default behaviour of most ticketing systems — an error, a cleared basket, and a fresh seat map — loses about a third of these guests. Offering three equivalent alternatives at the same price, with the one seat they still hold preserved, recovers 81% of them. The work is entirely in the seat attributes: without measured distance, steps and sightline there is no way to say what "equivalent" means.

Every self-service action is a call not taken
Date moves, seat changes and transfers were 61% of box office call volume before they were self-service. They are also the four things guests are most anxious about doing wrong, which is why each one states the rule and the consequence in the same sentence as the button.
