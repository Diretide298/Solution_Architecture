Three cannot be switched off, and the screen says why
A seat change, a cancellation and a refund are facts about money the guest has already handed over; sending them is not marketing and not optional. Listing them as always-on, in the venue's own accent, is more honest than burying them in terms and lets the three genuinely optional messages be trusted.

One a month means one
The marketing message is rate-limited in the platform, not in a campaign tool, so a tenant cannot send four in a week even if they want to. It is the one place where the venue's freedom is deliberately capped by us.

The notification carries the answer, not a tease
The lock-screen text names the new seats and says "same distance, no steps", so a guest who never opens the app still knows they are fine. A notification that says only "there has been a change to your booking" produces a phone call from everybody who reads it.

Same numbers as the emailed version of this notice, which is the point: one message, one set of words, whichever channel reaches them.
If any fact is worse
Further back, more steps, a split party or a lower band, and the screen says so at the top, states the refund of the difference plus 20%, and puts the refund button first. The app never describes a downgrade as equivalent.

The refund goes before the message
Making a guest request money back for a performance the venue cancelled is the cheapest possible way to lose them. The refund is issued first, the pre-order refunded with it, and the notification reports both as facts. The only thing asked of the guest is whether they want their seats held for a replacement date.

Two thirds asked to keep their seats, which is the number that decides whether adding a replacement performance is worth it.
Guests already in the building
Anybody who has scanned in is told by a person in the foyer, not by this notification — front of house gets their names the moment the cancellation is confirmed. The app is for the several hundred still at home or on a train.
Travel costs are offered on request without proof, and the line saying so is on the screen rather than in a policy.

No carousel, no permission prompts, no account wall
Most first launches happen within two hours of a performance the guest has already paid for, so the first screen has one obvious button. Notification permission is asked once a ticket is found, and access needs are never asked here — the app has no business raising that before it has done anything useful.
